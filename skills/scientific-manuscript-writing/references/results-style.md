# Results prose: evidence, interpretation and transitions

Use this reference when drafting a Results section, improving paragraph connections, or revising prose around figures. The observations below come from the supplied versions of four research articles. They are editorial readings of those papers, not rules prescribed by their authors. The sample sentences are original illustrations, not quotations or additional scientific findings.

## Patterns in the reference papers

| Source and passages | Observed writing pattern | Transferable practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Li et al. (2022), *Predicting the lattice thermal conductivity of alloyed compounds from the perspective of configurational entropy*, Results, article pp. 1–4; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41524-022-00771-1) | Introduces crystal structure and constituent configurations, develops the descriptor from a concrete alloy example, then checks prediction and transferability in separate subsections. Quantitative comparisons support each assessment of the method. | Introduce the physical system before the analysis when its structure motivates the descriptor. Explain a new quantity through its physical role and a concrete example. Separate accuracy within the studied set from transferability to another family. |
| Li et al. (2026), *Disentangling cation–polyanion coupling reveals which anion motion dominates cation transport in solid electrolytes*, supplied article-in-press version, Results, PDF pp. 2–5; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-77273-x) | Opens with structures, the conductivity observable and constraint conditions. States the trend across the material set before giving numerical examples. Later headings foreground physical findings about cooperative transport, frequency matching, and barrier/prefactor contributions. | Define comparisons once, state the shared trend, then identify meaningful differences. Use a finding-led subsection title when the evidence supports a clear conclusion. Keep qualifications attached to the quantity or comparison they affect. |
| Jun et al. (2025), *Exploring the soft cradle effect and ionic transport mechanisms in the LiMXCl4 superionic conductor family*, Results, article pp. 2–9 (PDF pp. 3–10); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matt.2025.102001) | Progresses from crystal structure and stability to conductivity and its mechanism. Differences in conductivity motivate the next analysis. Conditional probabilities are defined before their trends are interpreted; differences between materials then motivate a structural explanation. | Make the next analysis answer a question raised by the preceding result. Distinguish a measured correlation from the proposed physical explanation through the sentence's verb and subject. State a discrepant comparison directly when it changes the interpretation. |
| Smith and Siegel (2020), *Low-temperature paddlewheel effect in glassy solid electrolytes*, Results, article pp. 3–8; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-15245-5) | Establishes the structural model, identifies migration events, examines spatially adjacent ions and anions, then develops vibrational and energetic evidence. Representative events are described with explicit times, displacements and coordination changes. | Build a mechanism from connected observables. Move from temporal behavior to the relevant spatial arrangement, then to complementary evidence. Explain what a trace or configuration reveals, rather than only enumerating panels. |

Adopt these argumentative habits selectively. Do not copy every expression in the papers: repeated reader directives, promotional adjectives, long parenthetical qualifications and conversational questions need not become part of the manuscript's style. These articles also contain substantive qualifications; their presence does not justify adding a generic caution to every paragraph.

## Open sections and connect paragraphs by function

Choose the opening from the scientific relationship. A transition word alone cannot establish that relationship. Vary the construction and allow a concrete subject to begin a paragraph without an introductory phrase.

| Purpose | Useful construction | Original example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduce the material | Material-led statement | “The crystal structure comprises isolated tetrahedra separated by mobile-ion sites (Fig. 1a).” |
| Introduce an analysis | “To quantify…” or “To determine whether…” followed by the actual question | “To quantify the coupling between these motions, we compare their event times.” |
| State a finding | Quantity-led statement or “We find that…” | “The low-frequency intensity increases during migration events (Fig. 2b).” |
| Extend a result | “Furthermore”, “In addition”, or a direct new subject | “The same trend occurs across the remaining compositions.” |
| Explain an observation | “To account for this trend…” or “This behavior suggests…” | “To account for this trend, we examine the local coordination during migration.” |
| Compare alternatives | “By contrast” or “However”, with the contrast named | “By contrast, the constrained framework exhibits fewer migration events.” |
| Test scope | “To assess whether this behavior persists…” | “To assess whether this behavior persists at higher temperature, we repeat the event analysis.” |
| Synthesize evidence | “Together, these results…” followed by a specific conclusion | “Together, these results indicate coupled ion and framework motion.” |

Use “therefore” and “thus” for a supported inference, not merely the next calculation. Use “suggests” for an interpretation, “shows” for a directly displayed result, and “demonstrates” for a conclusion established by the stated evidence. These are contextual choices, not a rigid confidence ladder. Avoid stacked hedges such as “may potentially suggest”.

## Advance the scientific story in successive stages

Guide the reader through the reasoning that connects analyses. Before introducing the next panel, identify the specific question raised by the preceding result and explain what the new view will resolve. The progression can move from a macroscopic observable to individual events, from a mean response to its variation, or from a correlation to a separate energetic analysis. Preserve the actual evidential relationships; do not manufacture a causal test or a historical order of discovery.

For example, an average can reveal the timing of a response, while event-resolved maps show how that response is distributed across individual events. A suitable bridge is “Beyond this average response, we examine how the local rearrangement develops across individual events.” Follow it with the observed pattern, its variability and the supported interpretation. Do not simply append “Furthermore” to a second panel description.

Formal expressions can give this progression the author's preferred academic voice:

- “To examine whether ...” introduces a specific analysis question.
- “It is found that ...” reports the observation that answers it.
- “It is noted that ...” draws attention to a feature that matters for interpretation.
- “It should be mentioned that ...” emphasizes a consequential condition or result; it need not introduce a defensive disclaimer.
- “It should also be mentioned that ...” adds a distinct point whose relevance is stated.
- “On the other hand, ...” introduces a genuine complementary or contrasting behavior.
- “Beyond the average response, ...”, “We next examine ...” and “To explore this variation, ...” connect successive levels of analysis.
- “Taken together, ...” integrates complementary evidence into a specific scientific conclusion.

Use these expressions selectively alongside direct statements. Their role is to make the scientific reasoning visible, not to replace it or lengthen every sentence. Keep ordinary words and precise physical subjects even when using a more formal academic register.

## Check repetition across paragraph boundaries

Scientific progression should remain clear without repeating the same author-led sentence pattern. Review each paragraph together with its neighbors, including the last sentence before a new paragraph. Look for repeated subjects, opening clauses, main verbs, emphasis phrases and the same conclusion stated twice. Treat “We first assess ...” followed by “We next consider ...” as a repeated construction even though the verbs differ.

Let the next scientific subject carry the transition. For example, a paragraph on the relationship between a spectral indicator and displacement can be followed by “The analysis window also determines how finely the motion can be resolved in time.” This introduces the next methodological choice and its purpose without another “We ...” opening. Other passages may naturally begin with the material, a contrasting response, an unresolved question or a physical implication. These are options, not a fixed rotation of templates.

Preserve formal linking expressions and the author's step-by-step argument, but distribute them deliberately. Avoid replacing one repeated pattern with consecutive “Figure ...”, “To ...”, “It is found that ...” or “These results ...” sentences. Repetition of an essential technical term is acceptable when it preserves meaning; variation should come primarily from sentence structure, emphasis and removal of redundant reasoning.

## Give each sentence sufficient scientific content

Avoid a short, generic topic announcement followed by another sentence that finally supplies the comparison or evidence. Combine closely related ideas so that the purpose, observable and conditions form a coherent academic sentence. For example, replace “Displacement provides a direct test of the spectral indicator. The figure compares the two quantities.” with “To assess whether the spectral indicator tracks ion migration, the figure compares its intensity with the displacement of individual ions over the same trajectory window.” The revision adds a logical relationship rather than decorative wording.

Review the whole passage for abrupt introductory statements, isolated definitions and loosely connected observations. Integrate them with the relevant explanation while retaining varied subjects, clear antecedents and the step-by-step scientific argument. A short sentence that conveys a complete, important finding can remain; the issue is thin or disconnected prose, not a numerical minimum length. Avoid solving the problem through padding, excessive nominalization or several independent claims joined into one long sentence.

## Make the paragraph do scientific work

A figure discussion usually needs three ingredients: enough orientation to understand the observable and comparison; the principal trend with the relevant magnitude or conditions; and its physical meaning. These ingredients may span several paragraphs. Do not force every paragraph into three sentences or end each one with the same formula.

Orient the reader to the panels that carry the argument. Put exhaustive color keys, symbols, fitting details and uncertainty definitions in the caption or Methods unless they are needed for the immediate interpretation. A schematic explains a definition or proposed mechanism; measured or calculated data establish the finding. A representative event illustrates behavior, while an ensemble analysis establishes its prevalence.

End a paragraph with its substantive conclusion or a concrete connection to the next analysis. Replace generic endings such as “This provides a basis for further investigation” with the actual relationship being investigated. Do not add an unsupported mechanism merely to supply an implication.

## Direct phrasing without routine disclaimers

| Draft tendency | Preferred approach |
| --- | --- |
| “Conductivity was estimated using the Nernst–Einstein relation, without an explicit correction for correlations between different ions.” | “Conductivity was calculated from ion self-diffusion using the Nernst–Einstein relation.” Describe the estimator's treatment of correlations where its definition or a comparison requires it. |
| “This is only an ensemble tendency rather than an identical sequence in every event.” | State the actual statistic and scope, for example, “The event-averaged signal increases before migration onset.” Discuss event-to-event variation when it is quantified and relevant. |
| “These results do not by themselves prove a causal mechanism.” | State the supported result directly: “These results show a temporal correlation between the two motions.” Identify any unresolved causal question where that question motivates a test or affects the conclusion. |
| “The method provides a robust and reliable basis for the following analysis.” | Give the measured agreement, error or comparison that establishes performance. Do not substitute praise for validation. |

Removing defensive phrasing must preserve the observable and the evidence. Retain a real mismatch, uncertainty or sampling limit when omitting it would change the reader's scientific conclusion. Express it specifically and once, close to the relevant claim.
